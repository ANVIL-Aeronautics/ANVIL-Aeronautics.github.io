### AeroRover — Hybrid Ground-Air Mobility Platform

<p align="center">
  <img src="assets/aerorover-banner.jpg" alt="AeroRover prototype" width="100%">
</p>

AeroRover is my hybrid ground-air mobility platform.

The idea is to explore a machine that can move on the ground, handle constrained environments, and perform short aerial maneuvers when needed.

This is not about building another conventional drone.

It is about testing a new form of mobility between the ground and the air.

**Public execution record:**

- Ground driving demonstrated
- Initial low-altitude lift achieved
- Hybrid mobility architecture tested
- Redesign ongoing

The public version of this page intentionally omits detailed geometry, control logic, and mechanism-level design.

> AeroRover is my attempt to ask a simple question:  
> What if a machine could choose whether the ground or the air is the better path?

### Ornithopter — Bird-Inspired Aerial Platform

<p align="center">
  <img src="assets/ornithopter-banner.jpg" alt="Ornithopter prototypes and flight tests" width="100%">
</p>

I built a series of bird-inspired flapping-wing aerial prototypes.

This project became one of the hardest physical engineering grinds of my life.

**Public execution record:**

- 11 hardware iterations
- 15+ flight tests
- Personal funds invested
- No full-time team
- No corporate lab
- Repeated structural failures
- Repeated flight failures
- Continuous redesign

Most of the tests failed.

But they were not empty failures.

I learned through broken frames, insufficient lift, actuator limits, structural stiffness problems, manual tolerance issues, unstable attitude, inverted motion, and repeated rebuilds.

The public version of this page intentionally omits detailed dimensions, materials, actuation parameters, and control methods.

This project taught me that real aerospace engineering is not romantic.

It is physical, repetitive, violent, and honest.

> I did not have the ideal environment.  
> So I built anyway.

## Core Beliefs

Regulations, culture, credentials, age, lack of money, lack of a team, skeptical professors, and skeptical investors are real constraints.

But they are not final verdicts.

If something is truly necessary, I believe we should try to make it real.

If it does not work, change the method.  
If it still does not work, pivot.

But I do not accept the habit of declaring something impossible before it has been seriously tested.

## Builder Story

### 1. From Competition to Engineering Identity

I was highly competitive from a young age.

In middle school, I spent two years as the second-ranked student in my entire school.

Eventually, in my third year, I became first.

But what mattered most was not the rank.

I loved studying.

I hated losing, but I also genuinely enjoyed learning.

That combination pushed me hard.

Then I failed to enter a science high school.

That failure changed me.

It was not just a disappointment.

It was the first major event that made me question the system around me.

I began to move away from the path of becoming a conventional elite student.

I wanted to become someone who could see, think, build, and prove things directly.

---

### 2. Independent Curiosity

In high school, I became interested in questions outside the standard curriculum.

I wondered whether black holes could be related to cosmic expansion.

I studied aerospace topics and fluid mechanics on my own.

My curiosity was never only about knowledge.

It was about motion.

What flies?  
What flows?  
What collides?  
What changes trajectory?  
What invisible forces move physical bodies?

Those questions pulled me toward aerospace engineering.

---

### 3. The COVID Year: English, Philosophy, and a New Worldview

After failing my first college entrance attempt, I spent a year preparing again.

During the COVID year, I changed deeply.

I modeled COVID case numbers mathematically.

I studied aerospace, vaccines, security, earthquake prediction, fluid mechanics, and other technical subjects on my own.

I discovered Elon Musk and absorbed a first-principles way of thinking:

> If physics does not say no, keep looking for a way.

I also decided that I truly wanted to learn English.

For ten months, I wrote in English, spoke in English, watched English YouTube without subtitles, and read English newspapers.

Until the end of high school, I could barely speak English.

In university, I earned A+ grades in two English courses.

Today, I can make British students laugh in English.

During this period, I also became interested in philosophy, open societies, anti-totalitarian thinking, and the question of what technology should serve.

America matters to me not because it is perfect.

America matters because it is one of the few places where young builders can still believe they might change the world without waiting for permission.

That year transformed me from a student trying to enter a good university into someone who wanted to live as an engineer.

### 4. Seoul National University

When I entered Seoul National University, I studied hard and earned a strong GPA in my first two years.

But by my junior year, I began losing interest in conventional evaluation systems.

In an automotive engineering club, I pointed out technical issues that were ignored.

That experience taught me something important:

### 6. Real-World Evidence: Vehicles, Accidents, and Truth

I obtained a heavy commercial vehicle license and a traffic accident appraiser certification because I wanted to understand physical events on roads.

### 7. Valley of Death

In 2025, I attempted my first serious startup effort in hard-tech aerospace hardware.

Then I hit the valley of death.

I clashed with a co-founder I had known for ten years.  
I failed a government startup program.  
I received skepticism from mentors.  
I ran out of money.  
The team collapsed.

In three months, I lost 13 kilograms.

I suffered insomnia, anxiety, anger, and burnout.

At that time, I lost the joy of engineering.

I became obsessed with results, evaluation, and external approval.

But that crash did not end me.

It trained me.

I learned that vision is not enough.

You need the right people.  
You need boundaries.  
You need structure.  
You need the right battlefield.

---

### 8. Faith and Rebuilding

In May 2025, I began believing in Jesus Christ.

Faith helped me rebuild my operating system.

I began separating what I could control from what I had to entrust to God.

I returned to engineering.

In June 2025, I joined an ideathon without even thinking of it as a startup competition.

My team was stuck trying to build a business model around a microbial wastewater treatment technology — a field outside my background.

In 30 minutes, I synthesized the core structure, built the business model, and delivered the pitch.

We won first place.

That experience reminded me:

> In the right battlefield, I can understand structure fast and create results.

### 10. Reality Confirmed My Direction

At ADEX 2025, I saw a compact aerial payload concept displayed by a major Korean defense company.

It was disturbingly close to something I had imagined and pitched months earlier — concept, direction, and business logic.

That moment hurt.

But it also confirmed something important:

My technical instinct had not been absurd.

The problem was not the direction.

The problem was that I had allowed criticism to stop execution.

Later, I interned at a drone startup and contributed simulation work and 3D design support for an anti-drone system related to a defense project.

I also became connected to DARPA Connect.

These moments confirmed that my direction was hard-tech, defense, robotics, aerospace, public safety, and real-world physical systems.

The right environment for this direction exists.

I intend to find it.

I believed SNU Engineering needed to stop neglecting young hardware builders.

So I went to the Dean's office and argued that students with serious technical vision needed a different kind of support.

In 2026, during a program workshop, the Dean publicly referred to me as **Class Zero** of the SNU Entrepreneurial Innovation Program.

I was not merely a student who entered the program.

I was one of the people who argued that such a program needed to exist.
